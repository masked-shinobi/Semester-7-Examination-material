1. Photo album & Face detection

Photo album

A digital photo album is software that lets you upload, store, arrange, label and view photos (on your phone, in the cloud, or on sites like Facebook). It can be a standalone app, part of the operating system, or come with a camera.

Where CV helps: sorting, tagging, slideshows, cropping, red-eye removal, one-touch colour/light fix, cloud sync. Biggest use: finding faces automatically.

ExampleWhen Facebook draws a box around your friend’s face and suggests a tag, that is face detection (find the face) plus face recognition (say whose it is).

Face detection = “Where are the faces?”

Before we can say who someone is, we must first find where the face is and its size/shape. Four methods:

MethodSimple ideaExample
Feature-basedLook for face parts (eyes, nose, mouth) using edges, corners, blobs, ridges. Parts present in the right places = face.Two dark blobs above a nose-shaped edge → face.
Template-basedSlide a ready-made face “stencil” over the image; check how well it matches (correlation). Several templates handle different angles.A stencil matched at −45° means a tilted face.
Appearance-basedTrain on many labelled face and non-face patches. Scan small overlapping boxes and classify each one. Uses clustering (group similar things; needs lots of data) or neural networks (learn from many inputs).Sorting fruit into groups of apples, pears, strawberries.
Knowledge-basedUse human rules: “a face has two eyes, one nose, one mouth in this order”.Rule checklist.
RememberDetection = find the face. Recognition = name the face.

2. Face Recognition

Identifies a person by comparing their face with faces saved in a dataset. It always runs in 4 steps:

Image
1 Detectionfind face box
2 Alignmentstraighten & crop
3 Representationface → numbers
4 Matchingcompare scores
  • Detection: returns a bounding box (rectangle) for each face.
  • Alignment: scale and crop every face the same way using fixed reference points (landmarks like eye corners) so all faces line up.
  • Representation: turn pixels into a short list of numbers called a feature vector / template – a compact “fingerprint” of the face.
  • Matching: compare two templates → a similarity score. High score = likely the same person.
ExamplePhone face-unlock: camera finds your face → straightens it → makes your number-fingerprint → compares with the one saved at setup → unlocks if the score is high enough.

3. Eigenfaces (PCA)

Eigenfaces = a face-recognition method based on PCA (Principal Component Analysis). PCA keeps only the most important differences between faces and throws away the rest, so each face is described by far fewer numbers.

Everyday pictureTo describe students in a class you don’t list every detail; you say “height and hair length” – the two things that differ most. PCA finds such “most different” directions automatically. Those directions, drawn as faces, are eigenfaces.

Steps (with the tiny example from your notes)

  1. Collect M grayscale face images of the same size (e.g. 100×100).
  2. Flatten each image into one long column: an N×N image becomes N² numbers (100×100 → 10,000). Call it Γᵢ. Stack all as matrix A.
  3. Mean face Ψ = average of all faces = (Γ₁+…+Γ_M)/M.
    Example: Γ₁=[10,20,30], Γ₂=[20,30,40], Γ₃=[30,40,50] → Ψ=[20,30,40].
  4. Subtract mean (centre the data): Φᵢ = Γᵢ − Ψ. This removes what all faces share.
    Result: Φ₁=[−10,−10,−10], Φ₂=[0,0,0], Φ₃=[10,10,10].
  5. Covariance matrix C = A·Aᵀ (shows how pixels vary together). Here C is all 200s (perfect correlation). For real images C is N²×N² (huge), so use the small trick L = AᵀA (M×M) instead.
  6. Eigenvalues & eigenvectors: solve det(C − λI)=0. An eigenvector is a direction; its eigenvalue says how much variation lies along it. Example: λ=600 with vector [1,1,1] (main variation), λ=0 and 0 (no variation).
  7. Pick the top k eigenvectors with the biggest eigenvalues. They form the face space; these are the eigenfaces.
Why it helpsA new face is described as “mean face + a bit of eigenface 1 + a bit of eigenface 2…”. Only k numbers are compared, so it is fast.

4. Active Appearance Model (AAM) & 3D Shape Models

Active Appearance Model

An AAM fits a trained model of face shape + appearance onto a new photo. Shape = positions of eyes, mouth etc. Texture/appearance = pixel brightness inside the face. It is trained first on many example faces; the mean shape and mean texture are the starting point.

ExampleLike fitting a rubber mask on a stranger’s face: place it roughly, see where it doesn’t match, stretch it a little, repeat until it fits.
1 Placemodel on face (use face detection)
2 Measuredifference of pixels
3 Updatechange parameters
4 Iterateuntil difference tiny
  • Updates use optimisation like gradient descent or Gauss-Newton to reduce the difference.
  • Formula: c_new = c_old + Δc (c = parameter “sliders”, Δc = correction).
  • Algorithm: guess p → start at coarse (low) resolution (avoids wrong local fits) → measure error r(p) → predict fix δp = R·r (R = learned regression matrix) → p ← p − δp → repeat until stable, then go finer.

3D Shape Models (3D Morphable Model, 3DMM)

Uses 3D face geometry instead of flat 2D. A 3D database of face scans builds a morphable face model (shape + texture, each landmark has x, y, z) that can blend/interpolate between faces to create new realistic ones. Face analyzer reads a 2D photo; modeler builds the 3D face (2D input → 3D output).

FeatureAAM3D Shape Model
Data2D images3D facial scans
CapturesShape + textureShape (x, y, z)
TechniquePCA on shape & appearancePCA on 3D coordinates
Used forFace tracking, animation, synthesisPose-invariant recognition (works when head turns)

5. Surveillance: Foreground–Background Separation

Foreground = things that matter (moving person, car). Background = the unchanging scene. Separating them is used for intruder detection, traffic analysis and AR.

Basic idea: frame difference

Keep a picture of the empty scene. Subtract it from the current frame. Pixels that changed a lot are foreground. If |current − background| > Th (threshold) → foreground.

ExampleA CCTV sees an empty corridor. When a person walks in, only the pixels covering the person differ from the saved picture → the person lights up white in the mask.

Rules from the “Wallflower” study (1999)

  • P1 Find objects the moment they first appear.
  • P2 Define “stationary” per pixel; such pixels are background and ignored.
  • P3 Adapt to sudden (clouds, lights) and gradual (sunrise) changes; parked cars slowly become background.
  • P4 Handle change at different sizes (small leaf vs big object).

Frame differencing & threshold

Background = previous frame: B(t) = I(t−1). Foreground if |I(t) − I(t−1)| > Th. Works only for certain speeds/frame rates and is very sensitive to Th: low Th (25) = noisy but detailed; high Th (200) = almost nothing found. Choose Th by hand or automatically.

Pipeline

Video frames
Preprocessdenoise, grayscale, smooth
Background modelGMM, KNN, MOG2, deep
Foreground detectsubtract
Validateerode/dilate, size filter
Maskwhite=1, black=0

A Delay/buffer stores past frames so the model stays consistent and avoids false alarms.

Thresholding types

  • Simple: one fixed value for the whole image.
  • Otsu: picks the best value automatically; good when brightness has two clear groups (bimodal).
  • Adaptive mean: threshold from the average of each pixel’s neighbours.
  • Adaptive Gaussian: same but nearer neighbours count more.
gray = cv2.cvtColor(image, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
_, mask = cv2.threshold(gray, 100, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY)

6. Particle Filter

A particle filter (Sequential Monte Carlo) estimates where something is over time using many weighted guesses called particles. Use it when motion is non-linear, noise is not “bell-curve” (non-Gaussian) and you must track in real time (people in video, robot localisation, radar).

Example: car in fogYou can’t see the car clearly. You scatter many guesses of its position. As new blurry clues arrive, guesses near the clues get trusted more, the others fade, and the cloud of guesses follows the car.
InitialiseN particles, weight 1/N
Predictx = f(x_prev) + noise (move them)
Update weightsw ∝ p(z | x): how well it matches the observation
Normaliseweights sum to 1
Resamplekeep heavy, drop light
Estimatex̂ = Σ w·x

7. Chamfer Matching

Chamfer matching matches an object to a template using only their outlines (edges). (A chamfer is the bevelled edge of a block, hence the name.)

Preparation: segment the object → binarise (black/white) → detect contours (edge points).

Chamfer distance = for every edge point in the template, find the distance to the nearest edge point in the image, then average: D = (1/N_T) Σ DF_I(i).

ExampleLay a tracing of a star over a photo of a star. If outlines overlap exactly, distance = 0. The more the shapes differ, the larger the number.
  • Perfect match → 0. Computed in only N steps (N = template points). Works best with sparse points (a few hundred in 2D, few thousand in 3D); too few = inaccurate.
  • Problem: outliers (from occlusion or bad segmentation) wreck the score. Fix: truncated distance, min(DF, d), so no single point counts more than d.

Using it for tracking

  • Exact (Euclidean) distance is slow → Borgefors’ 3-4 distance transform estimates it cheaply, and a hierarchical (coarse-to-fine) search finds the best match.
  • Chen: simulated annealing for an optimal fit. Thayananthan: tracked hand shapes in clutter.
  • Rotation invariance: original method fails if the object rotates; eigen-decomposition on binary images fixes this.
  • Orientation Distance Transform (ODT): each pixel stores a complex number: size = distance, angle = direction to the nearest edge. Tells where the object is, not just how similar.
  • Likelihood: like = TPR × (1 − FPR) (true-positive rate vs false-positive rate).
  • Shift estimation: overlaying the mask on the ODT map gives a shift vector pointing to where the object moved.

8. Occlusion

Occlusion = part of an object is hidden by something else. Partial (some hidden) or complete (all hidden). Hard for trackers because the object suddenly looks smaller or vanishes.

ExampleA person walks behind a pillar; the tracker only sees half of them, or none.
1 Segment & colour-trackbox each coloured object
2 Specifystore area, width, height, coordinates per object
3 Repeat on all framesrecord Max_Area, Max_Width, Max_Height, biggest box
4 Comparedetect occlusion

Correction

  • Case I: two areas with the same mean colour appear (one object split by another in front) → replace the 2 boxes with one rebuilt box that tracks the hidden object behind.
  • Case II: width or height shrinks suddenly → the small box only covers the visible part, so replace it with a bigger box that covers the whole object, including the hidden part.
Good sideIn AR/immersive tech, handling occlusion lets a virtual sofa correctly hide behind a real table, so it looks real.

Also called multi-view vision or multi-camera fusion. Used in surveillance, motion capture, self-driving, 3D reconstruction, AR.

  • Intrinsic parameters: the camera’s own properties (focal length, optical centre).
  • Extrinsic parameters: where the camera is and which way it points in 3D.
  • Triangulation: each camera sends a ray through the matched point; where rays cross is the 3D position. All such points = point cloud; joining them = mesh.

10. Human Gait Analysis

Gait = the way a person walks; it is fairly unique, like a fingerprint. Gait analysis uses video/sensors to identify people, detect abnormalities, monitor activity. It works from a distance and without the person doing anything.

Background subtractionMOG2, KNN
Silhouette modellingcontours, pose estimation
Temporal analysisoptical flow, GEI
ML classificationSVM, CNN, LSTM
  • MOG2: each pixel is a mix of Gaussians over time; adapts to lighting; good for indoor/outdoor CCTV. cv2.createBackgroundSubtractorMOG2()
  • KNN: classifies pixels by nearest neighbours; better for complex, moving backgrounds. cv2.createBackgroundSubtractorKNN()
  • Skeleton / pose estimation: finds joints (knees, ankles, shoulders) with OpenPose, MediaPipe, HRNet.
  • Frame differencing: subtract consecutive frames to spot steps/stride cycles.
  • Optical flow (Farneback, Lucas-Kanade): arrows showing each pixel’s direction and speed.
  • Gait Energy Image (GEI): average of silhouettes over one walking cycle; bright = parts that move a lot (arms, legs), so it is one image that summarises how you walk.

11. In-Vehicle Vision System

A windshield camera helps the car stay in lane, plan its path and avoid leaving the road (used in self-driving and ADAS – driver-assist systems).

A. Locating the roadway (lane detection)

Bird’s-eye viewtop-down; gives curvature & position

  • ROI (region of interest): keep only the road area (a trapezoid), ignore sky.
  • Canny: edge detector. Hough: finds lines from edge points (each point “votes” for lines through it).
  • Kalman filter: predicts and smooths lane position frame to frame.
edges = cv2.Canny(blur, 50, 150)
lines = cv2.HoughLinesP(roi, 1, np.pi/180, threshold=50,

D. Locating pedestrians

Important for pedestrian-protection in self-driving cars. Train a model on features of a human body (head, arms, legs), then detect and track people. The notes use HOG + Linear SVM.

  • HOG (Histogram of Oriented Gradients): describes shape by the directions of edges in small cells; a standing person has a typical edge pattern.
  • Linear SVM: classifier that draws a line separating “person” from “not person”.
  • detectMultiScale slides a window over the image at several sizes (scale=1.05) and returns boxes (x, y, w, h); winStride sets the step size.
hog = cv2.HOGDescriptor()
hog.setSVMDetector(cv2.HOGDescriptor_getDefaultPeopleDetector())
(regions, _) = hog.detectMultiScale(image, winStride=(4,4), padding=(4,4), scale=1.05)
Quick revision mapFaces: detect → align → represent (eigenfaces/AAM/3DMM) → match. Surveillance: subtract background → track (particle filter, chamfer) → handle occlusion → add cameras → gait. Cars: lanes (Canny+Hough) → markings (colour) → signs (colour+shape+classifier) → pedestrians (HOG+SVM).
