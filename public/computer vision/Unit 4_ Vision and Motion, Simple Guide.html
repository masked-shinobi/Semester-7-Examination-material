1. The big picture

A camera photo is 2D: it loses depth (how far things are). 3D vision is the set of tricks a computer uses to get depth back. Motion analysis studies how things change between video frames. Together they power self-driving cars, robots, AR, and medical scans.

Example: Humans judge distance with two eyes (binocular vision). The brain compares two slightly different views. Computers need maths to do the same.

Passive uses only normal light (stereo, shading, texture, focus). Active sends its own light or signal (laser, structured light).

2. Methods for 3D vision (overview)

Stereo vision (passive)

Two cameras a small distance apart (the baseline, B) photograph the same scene. An object shifts between the two images; that shift is the disparity. Near objects shift a lot, far objects shift a little.

Z = (f × B) / disparity    disparity = x1 − x2
Example: Hold a finger close, close one eye then the other: it jumps a lot (big disparity). A far tree barely moves (small disparity).

3. Projection schemes

Projection = turning a 3D point (X, Y, Z) into a 2D image point (x, y). If we understand it, we can reverse it to find real 3D positions (needed for calibration, reconstruction, measurement, navigation).

TypeIdeaFormulaUse
OrthographicParallel rays. True size, depth lost.x = X, y = YEngineering drawings
PerspectiveRays meet at one point (pinhole). Far = smaller.x = f·X/Z, y = f·Y/ZCameras, AR/VR
Weak perspectiveAll points at about the same depth, so one scale s.x = s·X, y = s·YFaces, flat objects
StereoTwo views give disparity.Z = b·f / (x1 − x2)Depth sensing

Vanishing point: real parallel lines (like railway tracks) appear to meet in the image. Vanishing points on the ground line up to form the horizon line; they reveal whether a road is flat or sloped.

Lenses: a convex lens really forms an inverted image behind the lens. For easy maths we draw a non-inverted virtual image in front. The optical centre of the lens is the centre of projection.

4. Shape from shading

Idea: from one image, bright areas face the light, dark areas face away, shadows get no light. Brightness pattern tells us the surface tilt.

Example: A ball under a lamp: front is bright, sides dim, back dark. That smooth change shows the ball is curved, with no depth sensor needed.

Brightness depends on: light angle, how reflective the surface is, and the viewing direction. We assume a Lambertian surface (matte, reflects equally in all directions):

I = E × cos(i)   (E = light energy, i = angle between light and surface normal)

i = 0° means facing the light (brightest); i = 90° means sideways (dark).

Steps: grey image, assume light direction and Lambertian, measure each pixel brightness, compute surface normals, integrate normals to get 3D shape.

Use: faces, hills, smooth objects.

5. Photometric stereo

Idea: keep the camera fixed, change the light direction, take several images (3 or more). Comparing the brightness of the same pixel gives the surface normal N.

I = ρ × (L · N)   ρ = reflectivity (albedo), L = light direction, N = surface normal
Example: Photograph a coin lit from left, right, top. Each side goes dark or bright in turn, so the computer works out every bump and engraving.

p-q graph: p and q are the surface slopes in x and y. Each light gives a contour of possible (p, q); where all contours cross is the true orientation.

  • Good: one camera, very fine detail.
  • Limits: needs controlled light, matte surfaces, known light directions; shadows and shine cause errors.
  • Use: sculptures, dent detection, skin texture, face reconstruction.

6. Shape from texture

Idea: repeating patterns (tiles, bricks, grass) look bigger and spaced out when near, smaller and denser when far.

Example: Look at a brick wall at an angle: near bricks look big, far bricks small and squeezed. Your brain concludes the wall is slanted.
  • Size: near = larger. Density: far = more elements per area.
  • Foreshortening: patterns look squeezed depending on tilt. Orientation of the pattern shows rotation.

Needs visible, even texture; gives relative (not exact) depth. Use: terrain maps, road slope, robot ground detection, AR floors.

7. Shape from focus (and defocus)

Idea: a lens is sharp at only one distance. Take a focus stack (same scene, different focus settings). For each pixel, the image where it is sharpest tells its distance.

Steps: (1) capture stack, (2) compute a focus measure per pixel (variance, Laplacian, gradient), (3) pick the sharpest image, (4) build the depth map.

Example: Focus on a statue: front sharp first, then middle, then back. Note where each part is sharpest to get its distance.

Good: single camera, no special light. Limits: needs many images, fails on transparent or textureless surfaces, scene must stay still. Use: microscopes, quality control.

8. Active range finding

The sensor sends out its own signal (laser, infrared, sound) and measures the echo.

Distance = (speed of signal × time) / 2   (÷2 because it goes there and back)
  • Time-of-Flight (ToF): time a pulse's return. LiDAR, ultrasonic sensors.
  • Triangulation: a laser spot is seen by a camera; its angle gives distance. Short range, very precise.
  • Structured light: project lines/grids/dots; their bending shows 3D shape (Kinect).
Example: A bat shouts and times the echo. Same idea with light.

Good: works in dark and on plain surfaces, accurate, real-time. Limits: bright ambient light interferes, costs more.

9. Surface representations: point-based vs volumetric

Point-based (point cloud)

The shape is stored as thousands or millions of 3D points (x, y, z), sometimes with colour. No surfaces or connections are stored. Made by LiDAR, stereo, depth cameras, photogrammetry.

+ simple, detailed, fast to scan. − no surface (needs meshing), large, noisy.

Volumetric (voxels)

Space is cut into tiny cubes called voxels (3D pixels) that describe the inside as well as the outside.

  • Occupancy grid: each voxel is 1 (inside) or 0 (outside).
  • Signed Distance Function (SDF): each voxel stores distance to the surface; negative = inside, positive = outside, 0 = on the surface.
  • Octree: divides space only where needed, so empty areas save memory.
Memory example: 100×100×100 voxels at 1 byte = 1 MB. Double the resolution to 200³ and it is 8 MB. Memory grows with n³, so detail is expensive.

10. 3D object recognition

Identify what an object is and where it is (its pose = position + orientation) from depth maps, stereo images or point clouds.

Four approaches

  • Model-based: match against stored 3D models (gear, bolt, cup).
  • Feature-based: use edges, corners, planes; works even if partly hidden.
  • View-based: store several 2D views (top, side, front) and compare.
  • Deep learning: 3D neural networks learn from data (LiDAR cars, trees, people).

Six steps: acquire data, preprocess (remove noise, separate object), extract features, match, estimate pose, verify.

Techniques: geometric hashing (fast hash-table matching), 3D Hough transform (voting), ICP (Iterative Closest Point: repeatedly shift/rotate points until they align with the model), 3D CNNs.

Challenges: occlusion, noise, heavy computation, lighting/scale/rotation changes. Use: robot picking, self-driving, medical, AR, 3D face recognition.

11. Introduction to motion

Motion = change in position over time across video frames. It reveals direction, speed, even 3D structure.

  • Object motion: object moves, camera fixed (ball on floor).
  • Camera motion: camera moves, object fixed (drone over a building).
  • Combined: both move (filming a car from another car).

Represented by: a displacement vector (dx, dy) per point, or optical flow (a field of such vectors).

Estimated by: feature tracking (follow corners), optical flow, block matching (track image blocks), 3D motion from stereo.

Example: A point goes from (100,200) to (115,195) in 0.04 s. Displacement = (15, −5) px; velocity = (375, −125) px/s.

Challenges: motion blur, occlusion, lighting changes, complex motion. Uses: tracking, video stabilisation, gesture recognition, surveillance, AR, sports analysis.

12. Triangulation

Find a 3D point as the crossing of two lines of sight, one from each camera through the point in its image.

Z = (f × B) / (x1 − x2)   larger disparity = closer

You must know: camera positions (extrinsic), calibration such as focal length (intrinsic), and matching points in both images.

Example: f = 800 px, B = 0.25 m. Object A disparity 40 → Z = 5 m. Object B disparity 8 → Z = 25 m. Doubling depth halves the disparity.

Good: simple geometry, only two views. Limits: calibration errors, hard to match points, noise/occlusion. Uses: 3D reconstruction, robots, AR.

13. Bundle adjustment

The final polish of a 3D reconstruction. It tweaks both the 3D points and the camera parameters (position, orientation, focal length) so everything agrees. Each camera sees a bundle of light rays; we adjust them so rays meet properly.

Reprojection error = where a point actually appears in the image minus where the model predicts it.

Minimise Σ (observed − projected)²   (non-linear least squares, e.g. Levenberg-Marquardt)

Steps: start with rough estimates, project 3D points into each image, compute errors, adjust points and cameras, repeat.

Analogy: Fine-tuning a 3D puzzle: nudge pieces until all fit. Observed (320,240) vs projected (323,237): error (−3,3), size ≈ 4.24 px.

Good: very accurate. Limits: heavy computing, needs good starting guess. Used in Structure from Motion, SLAM, photogrammetry.

14. Translational alignment

Line up two images (or 3D scans) by only sliding them: no rotation, no resizing. Translation vector T = (tx, ty, tz).

I1(x, y) = I2(x − tx, y − ty)
Example: Feature at (50,80) in image 1 and (65,72) in image 2 → T = (−15, +8) slides image 2 onto image 1.

Methods: cross-correlation, phase correlation (Fourier), feature matching, ICP (for 3D). Uses: image registration, panorama stitching, motion size, SLAM. Fast and simple but fails with rotation, scaling or distortion.

15. Parametric motion

Describe the motion of a whole region with a few numbers (parameters) instead of every pixel separately.

[x′ y′] = A·[x y] + [tx ty]   (A: rotation, scale, shear; t: shift)

Parameters are found by minimising E = Σ[I2(x′,y′) − I1(x,y)]². Good for rigid things (cars, boxes), stabilisation, tracking. Cannot handle non-rigid motion (walking people, waving cloth).

16. Spline-based motion

A spline is a smooth curve joined from simple polynomial pieces, like a flexible ruler bent through key points. Give a few control points and the spline fills in smooth motion.

p(t) = Σ Bi(t) · Pi   (Pi control points, Bi basis functions)

Types: linear (straight), quadratic (degree 2), cubic (degree 3, most used). Example: linear spline from (0,0) to (6,4): at t = 0.5 → (3,2).

Good: smooth, compact. Limits: choosing control points, poor at sudden jerks. Uses: animation, tracking, camera paths.

17. Optical flow

For every pixel, how far and which way it moved between two frames: a vector (u, v). u = x-movement, v = y-movement.

Example: A ball moves 5 px right between frames: flow = (u, v) = (5, 0).

Brightness constancy

A moving pixel keeps the same brightness: I(x,y,t) = I(x+u, y+v, t+1). This gives the constraint equation:

Ix·u + Iy·v + It = 0

Aperture problem: one equation, two unknowns (u, v). Through a tiny window you only see motion perpendicular to an edge, so extra assumptions are needed.

  • Lucas-Kanade (local): assumes constant motion in a small neighbourhood; solves from nearby pixels. Good for small, smooth motion.
  • Horn-Schunck (global): assumes smooth flow across the whole image; gives a dense flow for every pixel.

Image differencing: subtract two frames to see what changed. It shows that something moved but not exactly which way, so optical flow is needed.

Good: pixel-level motion, velocity. Limits: noise, fast/large motion, occlusion, lighting changes. Uses: tracking, compression, gestures, robot navigation.

18. Layered motion

Real scenes have many things moving differently. Split the picture into layers, each with its own simple motion model.

I(x,y,t) = Σ Ml(x,y,t) · Il(x,y,t)   (M = mask, I = layer appearance)

Steps: split into layers, estimate each layer's motion, warp each layer, combine.

Example: Car layer moves (10,0): pixel (50,50) goes to (60,50). Two layers with masks 0.7, 0.3 and intensities 100, 200 give 0.7·100 + 0.3·200 = 130.

Good: handles occlusion, background removal, segmentation, compression. Limits: heavy computing, fails if layers look/move alike.

Quick revision

  • Depth tricks: stereo (disparity), shading (I = E·cos i), photometric (I = ρ L·N), texture, focus, range finding (d = vt/2).
  • Projection: orthographic (no depth), perspective (x = fX/Z), weak perspective (scale s).
  • Storage: point cloud (points) vs voxels (occupancy, SDF, octree).
  • Motion: triangulation → bundle adjustment → alignment → parametric → spline → optical flow → layers.
