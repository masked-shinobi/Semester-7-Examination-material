1. Basics: what is an image, and why process it?

An image is a grid of numbers

You see a picture. A computer sees a grid of tiny squares called pixels, and each pixel holds a number for its brightness or colour.

  • Grayscale: black = 0, white = 255, grays in between.
  • Colour (RGB): each pixel has three numbers: Red, Green, Blue.
Example: a 3×3 grayscale image as the computer sees it (darker cell = smaller number):
[255,0,0] Red[0,255,0] Green[0,0,255] Blue[255,255,0] Yellow[128,128,128] Gray

Why do we need image processing?

Raw images can be noisy (random speckles), blurred, too dark or bright, or full of useless detail. We process images to enhance quality, extract useful information (edges, shapes, textures) and prepare for later tasks such as face detection.

If we skip it: the computer may misread the image, miss important details, detection or tracking may fail, and storage is wasted on unnecessary data. Example: finding a face in a blurry photo may give a wrong answer or none.

Image processing = taking an image in, doing operations on it, and getting out either a better image or useful data (edges, objects, features).

Everyday examples: removing noise from a photo, finding object edges, changing brightness/contrast, converting colour to black and white, finding corners or shapes.

2. Classical filtering

A filter changes each pixel using the pixels around it (its neighbourhood). A small grid of weights called a kernel (or mask) slides over the image. At each spot: multiply the kernel numbers with the pixels under it, add them up, and write the result to the centre pixel. This is called convolution. Filters are used for smoothing, sharpening and edge detection.

Mean (average) filter: smoothing

Replace each pixel with the average of its 3×3 neighbourhood. This softens noise.

Example: the centre pixel of becomes (10+20+...+90)/9 = 450/9 = 50.
Bigger example: a 5×5 image with a bright spot in the middle. After averaging, the spot spreads out to its neighbours.

Centre: (50×8 + 100)/9 = 500/9 ≈ 56. Pixel (1,2): neighbours 0,0,0,50,50,50,50,100,50 give 350/9 ≈ 39. On big images the window slides over every pixel (borders need padding, i.e. extra pixels added around the edge).

Salt-and-pepper noise = random pure white (255, salt) and pure black (0, pepper) dots. Averaging reduces them but also blurs the picture. The Box filter is the same idea with equal weights.

Gaussian filter: smarter smoothing

Like the mean filter, but the centre pixel gets more weight and far pixels less (a bell-shaped kernel). It blurs and reduces Gaussian noise while keeping edges better than a plain mean.

all ÷ 16
Example 1: a flat image of all 60s, with a black pixel (0) at (1,1). Neighbourhood is 60s with a 0 in the centre: (60+120+60+120+0+120+60+120+60)/16 = 720/16 = 45. The black dot is pulled up from 0 to 45.
Example 2: a white pixel (255) at (3,3): (60+120+60+120+4×255+120+60+120+60)/16 = 1740/16 ≈ 109. The white dot is pulled down from 255 to 109.
Example 3: neighbourhood gives 960/16 = 60. The centre stays the same because the pattern rises smoothly.

Median filter: best for salt-and-pepper

No kernel numbers. Take a window, sort the values, pick the middle one, put it in the centre. Extreme dots (0 or 255) are never in the middle, so they vanish, and edges stay sharper than with the mean.

Example: window sorted: 1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9. Median = 5, so the centre 9 becomes 5.

Laplacian filter: edges and sharpening

Highlights places where brightness changes quickly (uses the second derivative, see section 4).

Sobel filter: edges with direction

Two kernels. Gx notices brightness change left to right (finds vertical edges). Gy notices change top to bottom (finds horizontal edges). Combine: strength = √(Gx² + Gy²), or the quicker |Gx| + |Gy|.

Example: left side dark (10), right side bright (200). Patch times Gx = (−10+200) + (−20+400) + (−10+200) = 760, a big number, so a strong vertical edge. Gy on the same patch is about 0 because nothing changes up and down.

Why filter first? It cleans noise and strengthens important features so later steps (thresholding, edges, detection) work better.

3. Thresholding

Thresholding turns a grayscale image into pure black and white by picking a cut-off value T: pixel ≥ T becomes white (255), pixel < T becomes black (0). It separates an object from the background, simplifies the image and makes later steps cheaper. Used in OCR (reading text), document scanning, medical images, barcodes/QR codes, face region isolation.

Typical workflow: filter to remove noise, then threshold to extract the regions cleanly.

Type 1: Simple (global) thresholding

One T for the whole image, e.g. T = 127. Works only when lighting is even and object/background contrast is clear.

First row: 45→0, 130→255, 200→255, 90→0, 150→255.

Type 2: Adaptive thresholding

T is calculated separately for each small region (using the local mean or a Gaussian-weighted local mean). Great for shadows or uneven light. You tune the block size and a constant C.

Type 3: Otsu’s method

Finds the best T automatically. It tries every T, splits pixels into two groups (dark, bright) and picks the T where the groups are most different (maximum between-class variance, which is the same as minimum within-class variance). Works best when the histogram has two peaks (bimodal).

A histogram counts how many pixels have each brightness (0 to 255).

Example: pixels 50, 52, 55, 200, 202, 205, 48, 50, 210. Two clusters: dark near 50 (5 pixels), bright near 200 (4 pixels).
For T = 100: mean of dark group μ1 = 51, bright group μ2 = 204.25; weights w1 = 5/9 ≈ 0.56, w2 = 4/9 ≈ 0.44. Between-class variance = w1 × w2 × (μ1 − μ2)² ≈ 0.56 × 0.44 × 23557 ≈ 5815. Repeat for other T values and keep the largest; here T = 100 separates the groups best.

Filtering vs thresholding: filtering works on neighbourhoods to enhance or suppress features (blur, sharpen, edges) and is usually done first. Thresholding works pixel by pixel and gives a binary image.

4. Edge detection

Thresholding splits regions but does not show their outlines. An edge is a place where brightness changes suddenly. Edges often mark object boundaries, texture changes, surface-direction changes and shadow borders. We use them to find outlines, extract features, reduce data, and help segmentation, recognition and tracking.

ThresholdingEdge detection
Separates object from backgroundFinds the outline
Compares intensityMeasures rate of intensity change
Output: black and white regionsOutput: thin lines
Good for simple shapesEssential for complex scenes

The maths in plain words

  • First derivative (gradient) = how fast brightness changes. Big value → edge.
  • Second derivative = how fast the change itself changes. It shows peaks at sudden changes (used by Laplacian).
  • Gradient strength G = √(Gx² + Gy²); Gx = change horizontally, Gy = change vertically. Direction gives edge orientation.

Edge shapes (Figure 5.1)

  • A Step: instant jump, like black to white. Ideal, used for comparison.
  • B Slanted step: a quick ramp. More realistic.
  • C Smooth step: gentle rise, from blur or poor focus.
  • D Planar: steady slope, like shading on a smooth surface.
  • E Roof: rises then falls, a narrow ridge.
  • F Line: a thin bright spike. Roof and line are special cases, not normally used in regular edge detection.

Real images look like B and C (noise, blur, lighting). A and D are extremes.

Operators (the “mini filters”)

An operator is a small kernel that slides over the image and reports strong differences between neighbouring pixels.

Sobel works locally: it looks only at the 3×3 patch under the kernel, then slides on. In the example, a window with two dark columns (10) and one bright column (200) gives 760 (section 2). The edge sits between those columns.

Canny edge detection (John F. Canny, 1986)

The most popular method: accurate, low error, clean thin edges.

1 Gaussian blur2 Gradient (Sobel)3 Non-maximum suppression4 Double threshold5 Hysteresis
  1. Noise reduction: Gaussian blur so noise is not mistaken for edges.
  2. Gradient: find edge strength and direction.
  3. Non-maximum suppression: keep only the strongest pixel across the edge, so edges become one pixel thin.
  4. Double thresholding: above the high threshold = strong edge; between low and high = weak edge; below low = thrown away.
  5. Hysteresis tracking: keep a weak edge only if it touches a strong edge. This avoids broken or false edges.

Parameters: low threshold, high threshold, sigma σ (how much blur). Strengths: high accuracy, good localization (right position), minimal response (one edge, not many), clean maps, works on complex images. Limits: sensitive to parameter tuning and heavier than simple methods.

Edge detection keeps the important structure while cutting data. It is one of the first steps in detection, tracking and segmentation.

5. Corner and interest point detection

Edges show shapes, but matching two photos needs points that are distinctive and repeatable.

  • Flat area: little change in any direction.
  • Edge: big change in one direction.
  • Corner: big change in two directions. Test: slide a tiny window in any direction from a corner and the pixels change a lot.

Interest points are a bigger family: corners, blobs, junctions, textured spots, anything distinctive enough to match between images. Uses: image stitching (panoramas), object recognition, tracking, 3D reconstruction. Algorithms: Harris, Shi-Tomasi, FAST, SIFT, SURF, ORB.

Template matching

Take a small patch (the template) containing a distinctive feature, slide it over the big image, and at each spot measure how similar the patch is (using SSD, sum of squared differences, or NCC, normalized cross-correlation). High similarity = a match. Simple, and works best when the pattern repeats almost exactly. Used for tracking, stereo matching, stitching, finding logos/letters, game bots that find a button, and image registration (aligning images).

Second-order derivative methods

First derivative finds edges. Second derivative measures curvature, which is high at corners and blobs. It uses the Hessian matrix H = [[Ixx, Ixy],[Iyx, Iyy]] (Ixx = second change in x, Iyy = in y, Ixy/Iyx = mixed). Look at its eigenvalues (two numbers describing change strength in the two main directions):

  • Both large → corner / interest point.
  • One large → edge.
  • Both small → flat region.

Examples: Hessian detector, Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) for blobs, Harris-Laplace and SIFT DoG (difference of Gaussians) for scale-invariant points (found at different zoom levels). Such points are stable and repeatable for matching.

6. Mathematical morphology

A set of shape-based operations, first made for black and white images and later extended to grayscale and colour. A small shape called a structuring element (SE), e.g. a 3×3 square or a disk, probes the image.

OperationPlain meaningEffect
ErosionPixel stays 1 only if all pixels under the SE are 1Shrinks objects, removes tiny specks
DilationPixel becomes 1 if any pixel under the SE is 1Grows objects, fills small holes, joins nearby parts
OpeningErosion, then dilationRemoves small objects, keeps main shape size
ClosingDilation, then erosionFills small holes and gaps

Dilation step by step

Place the SE on every pixel. If any 1 is under it, the centre pixel becomes 1.

Properties: expands white regions, bridges gaps, smooths edges, and the result depends on the SE shape and size. Used in: text recognition (repair broken letters), medical images (connect parts of organs), road/shape detection (join line segments), after edge detection (strengthen lines).

7. Texture

Texture is how brightness or colour varies across an area: the pattern of a surface, like wood grain, fabric, bricks, sand, grass, water. Basic repeated units are texels (texture elements). The arrangement, size, orientation and spacing of texels define the texture.

  • It describes how intensity changes, not a single brightness value. A perfectly uniform surface has no texture.
  • Patterns can be regular (ordered) or random.
  • It can be coarse, fine, smooth or rough, and depends on intensity variation rather than colour.
  • Helps recognize, interpret and segment regions of an image.

Approach 1: Autocorrelation

Compare the image with a shifted copy of itself. If the texture repeats, the match score rises again at regular shifts (peaks). The faster the score drops, the coarser the texture.

Weaknesses: poor at telling some natural textures apart; repetition fades with distance; unreliable for isotropic textures (look the same in all directions).

Approach 2: Co-occurrence matrix (Haralick, 1973)

Count how often pairs of brightness levels appear next to each other, at a chosen distance and direction. Rows and columns are intensity levels; each cell is a count. It captures local variation and repeatability and became the standard in the 1970s.

Example: in the sample matrix the cell at row 3, column 6 would mean “brightness 3 sits next to brightness 6” that many times. Statistics taken from it, such as contrast, homogeneity and entropy (randomness), are used for classification and segmentation.

8. The big picture

Filter: clean and enhanceThreshold: separate regionsEdges and corners: find structureMorphology: tidy shapesTexture: describe surfaces

Each step feeds the next: filtering improves quality, thresholding segments, edge and corner detection locate key structures, morphology refines shapes, and texture analysis describes surfaces. Together they turn raw pixels into information for object detection, classification and pattern recognition in the upcoming units.
